A new service arrives with whatever dashboard the platform team installed. It
has forty panels, most of them flat, all of them unowned. Three months later
nobody can answer the only question that mattered during the incident: was this
get slower, or was it always slow?

Four signals are enough, if you know the order to read them in.

## The reading order

**Rate.** How many requests per second? If this changed, the rest of the
investigation is about a traffic change, not a code change. A latency increase
during a 3x traffic spike is a capacity story, not a regression.

**Errors.** What fraction of requests failed, and what kind? Count by status and
by error class separately. "The dashboard went up" is a rate question; "5xx
tripled" is a correctness question. They have different causes.

**Duration.** The distribution, not the mean. A mean of 200ms can hide a
percentile at four seconds, and it is the percentile your slowest users
experience that generates support messages.

**Saturation.** How full is the constrained resource — connections, memory, a
thread pool, a queue depth? This is usually the cause, and it is the last
signal to look at because it is downstream of the other three.

Read in that order and you eliminate most hypotheses before you open a log
searcher.

## Averages hide the problem

If you keep one number, keep p95. It is a reasonable compromise between the
sensitivity of a high percentile and the noise of a true maximum.

What you should not do is average. A service where 95% of requests take 30ms
and 5% take 8 seconds has a mean of 415ms, which describes no request anyone
actually made. The interesting behaviour is always in the tail, and the tail is
where your least patient users are.

## Alerts should mean "act now"

Every alert that does not require action is training your team to ignore alerts.
The test is simple: if this fires at 3am, what does the person on call actually
do?

Page on user impact: error rate, availability, and latency beyond a threshold
that a customer would notice. Everything else — a slow batch job, a queue
backlog that has hours of headroom, a certificate expiring in thirty days —
belongs in a ticket or a dashboard, not a rota.

## Logs explain, metrics detect

Metrics are how you know something is wrong. Logs are how you work out what
happened. Keeping them separate means each stays cheap enough to keep.

Structured logs with a request ID are the minimum that makes an investigation
possible. Without correlation, a distributed trace is a set of unrelated
timestamps and you are reading them in the order they happened to be rendered,
which is not the order they happened in.
